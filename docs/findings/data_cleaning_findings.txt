This indicates that the model's performance for EXPR changes considerably with the time horizon. It may be detecting distress-related signals close to failure without identifying them reliably much earlier.

An important statistical limitation

Most company-by-horizon cells contain only one or two scored quarters. An AUC calculated from such small samples can change sharply when a single observation changes.

Therefore, the company-level table is most useful for identifying cases that deserve investigation. It is not sufficient to conclude that the model reliably predicts distress for any particular company.

Also, the confidence intervals in the company-level uncertainty table resample control companies while holding the failed company's quarters fixed. They capture uncertainty from control selection, but not the full uncertainty associated with observing different quarters or different failed companies.

Conclusion for Part 2: The model's effectiveness is heterogeneous. CONN, BIG and TUEM are promising cases; RAD is a clear warning sign; and EXPR highlights the need to examine how performance changes with the forecast horizon.

3. Part 3 — Can the score distinguish failed companies from under-pressure companies?

This is a more demanding and commercially meaningful test than distinguishing failed companies from healthy ones.

A healthy retailer may have very different financial characteristics from a distressed retailer. An under-pressure retailer, however, may share many of the same characteristics as a company approaching failure.

Your table compares the same score and the same quarters against three groups:

-   failed_vs_healthy

-   failed_vs_UP, where UP means under-pressure

-   UP_vs_healthy

The proposed ordering is:

\[ \text{Failed} > \text{Under-pressure} > \text{Healthy} \]

To support that ordering, all three comparisons need to discriminate in the expected direction, and the difference between the failed-versus-healthy and failed-versus-under-pressure AUCs needs to be sufficiently clear.

The strongest evidence is in the 3–6 month window

  -----------------------------------------------------------------------
  Comparison                               lev2 AUC       lev3 AUC
  ---------------------------------------- -------------- ---------------
  Failed vs healthy                        0.902          0.902

  Failed vs under-pressure                 0.471          0.450

  Under-pressure vs healthy                0.855          0.751
  -----------------------------------------------------------------------

The score separates failed companies from healthy companies very well in this window. It also separates under-pressure companies from healthy companies reasonably well.

However, the failed-versus-under-pressure AUC is below 0.50 for both variants.

That is a critical finding: the score appears to distinguish financial weakness from financial health much better than it distinguishes companies that ultimately fail from those that are under pressure but do not fall into the failed group.

For lev3, the healthy gap is 0.467, compared with 0.431 for lev2. The reported confidence interval for the lev3 gap is 0.11–0.83, which is above zero. That supports a difference between the two discrimination tasks in this window, although the underlying sample remains small.

What happens at longer horizons?

At 6–12 months, failed-versus-healthy AUC remains high at 0.854 for lev2 and 0.860 for lev3. Yet failed-versus-under-pressure discrimination is weaker: 0.538 and 0.650.

At 12–18 months, the results weaken further. By 18–27 months, the AUCs are close to 0.50 for several comparisons, and the uncertainty intervals are extremely wide.

The longer-horizon results do not establish the full proposed ordering.

What this means for the project

The score may be more suitable as a broad financial-risk indicator than as a standalone failure classifier.

It could help identify companies that are financially different from healthy peers, but further work is needed to determine whether it can reliably identify which under-pressure companies will progress to failure.

Conclusion for Part 3: The full ordering of failed, under-pressure and healthy companies is not established. The strongest evidence is for distinguishing failed companies from healthy companies, especially 3–12 months before distress. The more difficult failed-versus-under-pressure comparison remains a major limitation.

4. Part 4 — Leakage, real-time feasibility and selection bias

This is arguably the most important part of the entire analysis because it asks whether the model could work in practice, using information available at the time a decision would be made.

4.1 Data leakage: encouraging result

Your check finds that 0 of 64 failed observations use inputs that first became public after the distress date.

That is good. It suggests that the tested failed-company observations do not contain the specific form of future-information leakage checked by this test.

However, this does not eliminate every possible source of leakage or bias. You still need to confirm that the same timing rules are applied to all controls and that the data definitions themselves do not encode information about future outcomes.

4.2 The score is not available at quarter-end

The median time between quarter-end and publication of all required inputs is:

  -----------------------------------------------------------------------
  Company group                Median reporting delay
  ---------------------------- ------------------------------------------
  Failed                       39 days

  Healthy                      33 days

  Under-pressure               39 days
  -----------------------------------------------------------------------

This means a score based on those inputs generally cannot be calculated immediately at quarter-end.

The actual lead time is therefore shorter than the quarter-end-based horizon suggests.

For example, your median lead time for the 3–6 month group falls to approximately 3.0 months after the inputs become public.

That is still potentially useful, but it changes how you should describe the model: it is a warning based on publicly available financial information, not necessarily a warning available at quarter-end.

4.3 The strict real-time results are mixed

Your real-time comparison restricts controls to those whose inputs were already public at the relevant prediction date.

For lev2, the results are:

  ------------------------------------------------------------------------
  Horizon                Fixed-score AUC           Real-time AUC
  ---------------------- ------------------------- -----------------------
  3–6 months             0.702                     0.694

The results are not uniformly worse under real-time restrictions. The 12–18 month AUC actually increases.

That does not necessarily mean real-time evaluation improves the model. The eligible controls and number of represented failed companies change between evaluations.

In particular, only five companies contribute in the 3–6 month window, seven in the 6–12 month window, six in the 12–18 month window and four in the 18–27 month window.

The 18–27 month result of 0.395 is concerning, but it is based on just four companies. It is not sufficient to conclude that the score systematically reverses its predictions at that horizon.

Important analytical check: before interpreting the real-time AUC differences as changes in model quality, verify that each comparison uses the same failed-company observations and a clearly documented eligible control set. Where the failed companies differ, the difference combines changes in evaluation population with changes in performance.

4.4 Company size may be a major source of selection bias

This is one of the most striking results in the output.

The median four-quarter sales figures are:

  -----------------------------------------------------------------------
  Group                               Median sales
  ----------------------------------- -----------------------------------
  Failed                              2,182 million

The healthy controls are dramatically larger than the failed companies.

Your size-only diagnostic shows:

-   Size alone versus healthy companies: AUC 0.978

-   Size alone versus under-pressure companies: AUC 0.596

-   Size alone versus both groups: AUC 0.787

A model that uses only company size achieves almost perfect discrimination between failed and healthy companies in this sample.

That is a serious warning. It suggests the strong failed-versus-healthy AUC may partly reflect differences in company size rather than distress-specific financial signals.

For example, if failed retailers are generally smaller than the selected healthy retailers, a score that ranks smaller companies as riskier could appear highly effective even if it does not identify the financial deterioration that precedes failure.

This does not prove that your score is merely measuring size. It shows that size is a powerful alternative explanation that must be tested before the score's performance can be attributed to distress signals.

I would next compare failed companies with healthy retailers of similar size, and ideally similar business models and reporting periods. You could also test whether the score retains discrimination after controlling for size.

The table also raises a question about whether the model is capturing financial distress, differences in company characteristics, or a mixture of both.

5. A technical issue to resolve before reporting the results

There is one inconsistency worth checking.

In Part 1, the 12–18 month lev2 results under the blanked-period analysis have 12 observations and a pooled AUC of 0.638, while the company-mean AUC is 0.643.

In the unblanked results, there are 13 observations, a pooled AUC of 0.666, and the same company-mean AUC of 0.643.

The identical company-mean AUC despite a changed sample may be perfectly legitimate, but it warrants verification. The additional observation may change the pooled AUC without changing the average of company-level AUCs.

More importantly, make sure that all tables consistently distinguish between:

-   Pooled AUC: calculated across the scored observations.

-   Company-mean AUC: the average of the company-specific results.

They answer related but different questions. With unequal numbers of quarters per company, the pooled result may give greater weight to companies contributing more observations.

Also, your confidence intervals are wide. For example, the blanked-period lev3 3–6 month interval runs from 0.53 to 0.91. This indicates substantial uncertainty about the true level of discrimination, despite the point estimate being 0.743.

6. Overall assessment

What the results support

-   Promising discrimination in the 3–12 month windows, particularly for lev3.

-   No evidence of the specific post-distress-publication leakage tested in the 64 failed observations.

-   Some failed companies are consistently easier to distinguish from controls than others.

What remains uncertain

-   Reliable predictive performance beyond 12–18 months.

-   Whether the score can distinguish eventual failures from under-pressure companies.

-   Whether company size and control selection inflate the apparent AUC.

-   Whether real-time performance generalizes beyond the small number of companies evaluated.

7. What I would prioritize next

My recommended order is:

1.  Match healthy controls by company size. This directly tests whether the strong healthy-only AUC is driven partly by size differences.

2.  Focus on the under-pressure comparison. Investigate why BGFV and PLCE have reverse-direction AUCs and why the score struggles with other under-pressure companies.

3.  Validate the real-time evaluation design. Confirm the exact prediction dates, eligible controls, and failed-company samples used in every horizon.

4.  Report uncertainty and company-level variation. Avoid treating small-sample AUC estimates as precise measures of performance.

5.  Keep the longer-horizon claims conservative. The current evidence does not establish a dependable two-year warning system.

8. Report-ready conclusion

Summary of Findings: Corporate Distress Score

The analysis provides preliminary evidence that the distress score can discriminate between failed companies and comparison companies, particularly within 3–12 months of distress. Under the blanked-period analysis, lev3 achieves company-mean AUCs of 0.743 and 0.732 in the 3–6 and 6–12 month windows, respectively. Performance weakens at longer horizons, and the results do not establish a reliable two-year warning capability.

Company-level results reveal considerable heterogeneity. CONN, BIG and TUEM show relatively strong discrimination under both variants, whereas RAD performs poorly. The small number of observations per company and horizon limits the reliability of these individual estimates.

The control-group analysis indicates that failed companies are easier to distinguish from healthy companies than from under-pressure companies. This suggests that the score may identify broad financial differences more effectively than it identifies which financially challenged companies will ultimately fail.

The leakage check is encouraging: none of the 64 failed observations uses inputs first published after the distress date. However, publication delays shorten the effective warning horizon, and the strict real-time results remain uncertain because the eligible control sets and represented failed companies vary by horizon.

A further concern is company-size selection bias. A size-only diagnostic achieves an AUC of 0.978 against healthy controls, suggesting that the strong healthy-only results may partly reflect differences in company size. Size-matched controls and further validation against under-pressure companies are therefore important next steps.

Overall, the score is a promising exploratory distress indicator, especially at shorter horizons, but its predictive value and real-time generalizability remain unproven. Further testing should prioritize control-group comparability, company-size effects, uncertainty estimation, and performance against under-pressure companies before the score is presented as a validated early-warning model.

My bottom line: your most defensible claim today is that the score shows promising short-term discrimination, particularly with lev3, but the apparent performance is sensitive to which companies serve as controls. The next major test is whether that signal survives size-matched comparisons and a strict, reproducible real-time evaluation.

3. Real-time design (17B Parts 1–4)

The audit shows the strict real-time check I built is not a real-time test.

-   Calendar alignment. The score compares each company with controls in the same calendar quarter. PRTY (December quarter ends), TUEM (also calendar quarter ends) and RAD’s last two quarters are compared with controls whose quarters end a month later, which hadn’t reported yet (n_public 0–2). So the strict version covers only 5–6 companies per bin. In the 3–6 bin those are BBBY, BIG, CONN, EXPR and JOAN. PRTY, RAD and TUEM drop out.

-   Eligibility. DBI is never public in time, WOOF only 30%, TGT 42%, COST 50%.

-   Late public dates. PRTY 2020-12-31 and RAD 2021-08 and 2021-11 first appeared as comparatives 10–14 months late. All four are unscored (COVID window), so no AUC is affected.

-   The 18–27 bin is untestable. Only 10 of 24 observations are scored, from 4 companies. Other horizon bins have 12–16 scored observations.

-   Strict AUC against the main method. 0.66–0.75 against 0.64–0.80, similar, with no sign that control timing inflates the main result. It rests on subsets, so I’m not calling it confirmation.

-   A proper real-time version would score each company at its actual public date against every control’s latest public quarter. That needs a different alignment from the one the score was built on, so I’d declare it as a separate variant before running it.
